Lesson 4: The four-chord loop, heard then played

Lesson 4 of 33 · L6 · Popular music

  • Bring: Your instrument

Learning intention

We are learning to hear a chord progression and play it back.

Outcomes MU5-LIS-01 MU5-LIS-02

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

In classWhat we do

  1. In the roomThe four-chord loop is heard across a set of songs before it is named. You work out that they share something, then you are told what it is: I, V, vi, IV.
  2. With the bandThe loop is sung first and then played, in two keys, so that it stays a shape in your hands rather than a set of letters on a page.
  3. On your ownFind by ear where the loop starts again, and mark the change points on your chart.

  1. 0:08 into With or Without YouThe bass arrives playing the whole loop on its own: D, A, Bm, G, one bar each. This is the clearest the four chords ever get, so start your counting here.
  2. 0:26 into With or Without YouBar one again. The loop comes round every eight and a half seconds, so it restarts again at 0:35, at 0:44 and at 0:52.
  3. 0:52 into With or Without YouWell into the song now, and the chords have not moved once. Everything that has changed has been layered over the top of them.
  4. 1:44 into With or Without YouThe biggest the arrangement gets. Same four chords, same eight and a half seconds. Nothing underneath has changed at all.
  1. 0:00 into Someone Like YouThe piano has the loop before anything else happens: A, E, F#m, D. Faster than the U2, so the loop comes round every seven seconds.
  2. 0:14 into Someone Like YouBar one, and the singing starts on the same beat. This is the only time in the song they line up, which is exactly why you cannot use the voice to find the loop.
  3. 0:35 into Someone Like YouBar one. Listen for the second chord: the bass note walks down instead of jumping, and the chord is still V.
  4. 1:03 into Someone Like YouStill the same four chords. This song gets bigger by adding to the loop, never by changing it.

LearnI, V, vi and IV: what the numbers are doing

Every major key has seven chords built on its seven notes, and they are numbered from the bottom up in roman numerals. In C major, chord I is C, chord V is G, chord vi is A minor and chord IV is F. Change key and every letter changes, but the numbers do not: in G major those same four chords are G, D, E minor and C. That is the whole reason for numbering them. A letter tells you what a chord is called. A number tells you what it is doing in the key.

Each of the four has a job you can hear.

  • I is home. It is where the loop feels settled, and where a song most often stops.
  • V pulls. It is the most restless of the four, and it wants to fall back to I.
  • IV moves away, but without much tension, which is why it can sit under a chorus for a long time without becoming uncomfortable.
  • vi is the minor one. It is I with the light off: it shares two of its three notes, so it sounds like home and feels nothing like it.

Put them in that order, I, V, vi, IV, and the loop leaves home, pulls, darkens and drifts, then starts again. It never quite closes. That is why it can run under a whole song without wearing out, and it is why you have heard it under so many songs already without noticing that it was the same four chords.

Hearing where it starts again

Do not try to count the chords. Count four, steadily, and listen for the moment the harmony brightens and settles. That is I, and that is bar one of the loop. The hard part is that a singer will often come in partway through, so the start of the singing and the start of the loop are rarely the same moment. Follow the chords, not the voice.

Worth keeping

The same small pattern sits underneath a great many songs.
