LearnHearing a blues before you can see the chart

The twelve-bar blues is built from just three chords, I, IV and V, moved through in a fixed order every time. Once you know the order, counting a chorus stops being guesswork: it becomes counting to twelve, in fours, four times.

What tells you it’s a blues in the first two bars

  • The chord itself, not just the note. Chord I is usually a dominant seventh, not a plain major triad — there’s a flattened seventh built into home itself, which is already a blues sound before anything else happens.
  • Blue notes. A flattened third or fifth leaning against the major chord underneath, played as an inflection rather than a clash.
  • The shuffle. Quavers played unevenly, long-short rather than even, so the groove has the same lean as the blue notes.
  • The turnaround. The last two bars almost always do something recognisable to point back to bar one, even before you’ve heard where bar one was.

None of this needs the chord chart in front of you. They are things a listener can hear inside two bars without it, which is exactly the skill this lesson asks for: recognise the genre by its features, then count the form underneath it.

Worth keeping

A genre is recognisable because of features that can be named.
