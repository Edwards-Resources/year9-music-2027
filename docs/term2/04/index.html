The long version

Same numbers, any key

You already know that a roman numeral names what a chord is doing rather than what it’s called — chord I is home, wherever home happens to be. The blues uses three of those numbers: I, IV and V.

  • I is home. Chord one, built on the key note. Where the form starts and where it always lands.
  • IV moves away, but gently — built a fourth above home, close enough that it never feels like it has left for good.
  • V pulls. Built a fifth above home, it is the chord with the strongest urge to resolve back to I, which is exactly why the form uses it right before the turnaround.

Change key and every letter name changes — I in C is C, I in G is G — but the numbers do not, because they describe a relationship, not a pitch. That is the whole reason a band can call “blues in Bb” and every player instantly knows the form without it being written out again: the numbers, not the letters, are what everyone already has memorised.

Labelling a progression is just naming each chord’s number instead of its letter, and playing the same progression in a new key is the proof you actually understand the relationship, rather than just the shape under your fingers in one key alone.

Worth keepingNaming a chord by its function is what lets a form move between keys.
