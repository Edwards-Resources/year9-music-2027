LearnA defensible inference, not a guess

A context sentence and a guess can look identical on the page. The difference isn’t confidence, it’s whether the claim is actually supported by something in the sound.

  • Start from a specific feature, not a general impression. “This sounds old” is a guess; “the recording quality and the acoustic instrumentation suggest an early recording” is a claim built on something you can point to.
  • Name the kind of context, so the marker knows what you’re claiming: style, culture, history or era, or the social or geographic setting the music suggests.
  • Stop where the evidence stops. A context sentence that goes further than what you actually heard, naming a specific date, a specific artist, a specific place, without evidence for that specificity, turns a defensible inference back into a guess, just a more detailed-sounding one.

The test is simple: could you point to the exact sound that led you to the claim? If yes, it’s an inference. If the honest answer is “it just sounds like it”, it’s a guess wearing an inference’s sentence structure, and a marker can tell the difference even when you can’t.

Worth keeping

A context claim has to be supported by the sound, not by a hunch.
