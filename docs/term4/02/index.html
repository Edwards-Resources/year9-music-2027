Lesson 2: Leitmotif: an idea attached to something

In classWhat we do

  2. In the roomTwo screen works are examined for how the same idea is transformed: what is held constant so it stays recognisable, and what is allowed to move. Interstellar first, then Howl’s Moving Castle.

LearnWhat has to survive for a leitmotif to still mean the same thing

A motif becomes a leitmotif the moment it gets attached to something specific — a character, a place, an idea — and is changed as that thing changes. The attachment is what makes it mean something, rather than just being a tune that keeps coming back.

  • Something has to stay constant. A rhythm, an interval shape, a contour, whichever feature you choose, it has to survive every transformation, or the audience loses the thread and the leitmotif stops working.
  • Everything else is free to move. Tempo, orchestration, harmony, dynamics, even the key, can all change around the constant feature without breaking the attachment.
  • The transformation has to mean something. A leitmotif slowed and darkened when its character is in danger is doing a job. The same change for no dramatic reason is just variation with no attachment behind it.

The test of a leitmotif is whether a listener who doesn’t know the plot could still guess that this is the same idea, changed, rather than a new idea that happens to share a few notes. Keep the constant feature simple enough to survive four or five transformations, and specific enough that it couldn’t be mistaken for something else.

Worth keeping

The same idea, changed, is how music tells a story over time.
